Peer-reviewed

Multilingual Mathematical Reasoning: Advancing Open-Source LLMs in Hindi and English

AAAI 2025 · Oral Anand, Prasad, Kirtani, et al. · 13 citations on Scholar

Why: Strong LLMs reason well in English math but stumble in Hindi — a language spoken by hundreds of millions. If small open models can’t reason across languages, they can’t serve the students who need them most.

How it works · Impact · Journey

How: Bilingual curriculum learning — train on progressively harder problems in both languages — plus a decomposition strategy that splits complex arithmetic into manageable steps, and rehearsal-based learning to avoid forgetting English while learning Hindi.

Impact: An 8B model outperformed Gemini 1.0 Pro on English and Hindi math in the paper’s evaluation, and bilingual training matched single-language training — one model, two languages, no trade-off.

Journey: This was my first lesson that how you train matters more than how big you train. It set up everything after: curriculum + RL in IRIS, and efficiency at AMD.

Paper: arXiv:2412.18415

ReviewEval: An Evaluation Framework for AI-Generated Reviews

EMNLP 2025 · Findings Garg, Prasad, Singhal, Kirtani, Mandal, Kumar · 29 citations on Scholar · Equal-contribution in arXiv v1

Why: Submissions are exploding, reviewers are scarce, and AI reviews are already being used — but often superficial, hallucinating, or unactionable. We need a way to tell a good AI review from a bad one before trusting it.

How it works · Impact · Journey

How: Two parts. ReviewEval — a framework that scores AI reviews on alignment with human judgment, factual accuracy, analytical depth, actionability, and adherence to venue guidelines. ReviewAgent — a generator with a venue-alignment mechanism and a self-refinement loop, plus an external loop that uses ReviewEval scores to improve the final review.

Impact: ReviewAgent improved actionable insights and depth over prior AI baselines and, on those dimensions, over expert reviews in the paper’s comparison — while giving the field standardized metrics instead of vibes.

Journey: This taught me that evaluation is the product. If you can’t measure hallucination and shallowness, you can’t fix them. That idea recurs in my multi-agent critiquing work.

Paper: EMNLP Findings 2025 · arXiv:2502.11736

IRIS: Interleaved Reinforcement with Incremental Staged Curriculum for Cross-Lingual Mathematical Reasoning

ACL 2026 · Long Gupta, Vyalla, Anand, Kirtani, Cambria, et al.

Why: Curriculum learning helps, but models still produce inconsistent step-by-step reasoning — especially when transferring from English to Hindi/Marathi. The missing piece is how much guidance to give at each step, not just how hard the problem is.

How it works · Impact · Journey

How: A two-axis framework: SFT on progressively harder problems (vertical) interleaved with reverse-curriculum RL that gradually removes step-by-step guidance (horizontal). A composite reward — correctness, step-wise alignment, continuity, numeric incentives — optimized with GRPO. New dataset CL-Math: 29k step-annotated problems in English, Hindi, and Marathi.

Impact: Consistent gains on multilingual math benchmarks, with the largest gains in low-resource and bilingual settings — +15.8% on the hardest Hindi math problems in my resume summary — and modest gains in high-resource languages.

Journey: The sequel to AAAI 2025. Curriculum alone wasn’t enough; we needed RL that teaches independent reasoning, not just copying steps.

EHR Representation Learning for PAD Amputation Risk

Unpublished project Medical AI · EHR · UCSD

Why: Amputation in peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is devastating and rare — ~0.63% in 6 months in this cohort. Catching high-risk patients early could enable interventions that prevent it. But EHR data is messy, sparse, and imbalanced: the wrong representation buries the signal.

How it works · Impact · Journey

How: Systematic comparison holding cohort, labels, and temporal split constant. Axes isolated: feature enrichment (labs, drug duration, recency), temporal structure (GRU over visit sequences), and local co-occurrence (CNN over concept sequences with Word2Vec). Metrics: AUC-PR (primary, for extreme imbalance), AUC-ROC, Brier, sensitivity at 90% specificity.

Impact: Temporal order (+104% AUC-PR) beat feature enrichment alone (+52%). Combined gains were sub-additive (0.348 vs 0.425 if additive) — temporal structure already captures some severity. No single model won everything: CNN best sensitivity, enriched GRU best AUC-PR, enriched LR best calibration. Next: heterogeneous GNN for patient-similarity as a third signal axis.

Journey: My first medical-ML project where “more features” wasn’t the answer. It changed how I think about EHR: order is signal. This is the work I want to continue in a medical lab.

Agentic Resilience: Multi-Agent Systems in Disaster Simulations

CSE 291 · A+ UCSD · Team of 5

Why: Disaster response needs 2,000+ agents coordinating under fire, smoke, and failing comms. Central planners blow up in tokens; agents that stick to stale plans die. We asked: can hierarchy + critiquing keep them alive and coordinated?

How it works · Impact · Journey

How: Robust Region-Based Adaptive Hierarchy — split the map into regions, each with a regional leader running CAMON/HMAS locally; a global leader redistributes idle agents on demand. Vigilant Critiquing — every h steps, a critic audits the remaining plan against fresh perception; if HAZARDOUS, halt and replan. Stress-tested with a 5-point torture test: packet loss, command failure, fog-of-war, data decay, indecision.

Impact: +81% spatial reasoning, +58.6% plan adaptation (hierarchy), +12–14.6% plan adaptation in suppression tasks (critiquing). But stress tests were sobering: 30% packet loss and 50% command failure collapsed coordination — a warning for real deployment.

Journey: The most systems-y project I’ve done. It taught me that resilience isn’t a metric you add at the end — it’s the architecture.

Schema Linking with Supervised Fine-Tuning

CSE 234 · Top 5 / 150 Data-centric ML

Why: Text-to-SQL fails before SQL even starts if the model links the question to the wrong tables/columns. With huge, cryptic schemas (SAP codes, NTSB), the model can’t see the right columns — or hallucinates ones that don’t exist.

How it works · Impact · Journey

How: Qwen3-1.7B + LoRA (r=8, q/v proj). 14 ablations: base-model choice, LoRA capacity, question-aware ordering, PK/FK vs type annotation, SAP pruning, synthetic augmentation, and max sequence length. Final: PK/FK for most schemas, type annotation for NTSB, synthetic examples for SAP + NTSB, 9k context, strict JSON parsing + canonicalization that drops hallucinated tables/columns.

Impact: Leaderboard 0.462 → 0.729. Biggest wins: base-model choice and longer context — not bigger adapters. Paraphrasing and naive oversampling hurt; targeted synthetic data that taught new schema patterns helped.

Journey: A masterclass in data-centric ML: the bottleneck wasn’t the model, it was what the model could see and what data it had seen.
